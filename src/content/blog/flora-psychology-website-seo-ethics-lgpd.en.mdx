## Conversion changes when many common marketing tactics are inappropriate

A psychology website has the same practical goals as other service sites: explain the work, answer questions, be found in search and make the next step easy.

But the constraints are different.

In [Flora Psicologia](https://lagrotti.dev/projetos/flora-psicologia), I could not treat health-related communication like an aggressive landing page full of guarantees, urgency tricks and social proof copied from e-commerce.

Brazil's psychology councils provide specific guidance around professional advertising, identification and the way services are promoted. Health information also receives special treatment under Brazil's LGPD.

So the design problem became more interesting: **how do you reduce uncertainty and help a person take action without turning vulnerability into a conversion hack?**

## Trust became a content architecture problem

Instead of relying on louder CTAs, I organized pages around questions a potential patient is likely to have:

- who is the professional?
- what is their registration?
- what kinds of service are offered?
- how does online or in-person care work?
- how does scheduling work?
- what should someone expect from the first contact?
- what information does the site collect?

This is good UX and good SEO for the same reason: it makes the page answer a real information need.

## Professional identification should be easy to find

A registration number hidden in a footer technically exists but does not communicate much trust.

I prefer making professional identity visible in the places where a person evaluates whether to contact the service.

The point is not decorative authority. It is verifiability.

For high-trust services, clear identity is often more persuasive than exaggerated marketing language.

## SEO does not require clickbait

I write around concrete search intent instead of generic "ultimate guide" titles.

A useful article can answer one clear question thoroughly, explain limits and point to primary sources when regulation matters.

That creates a better content system:

```txt
search question
      ↓
useful page
      ↓
clear professional context
      ↓
optional next step
```

The CTA becomes the end of an explanation rather than an interruption.

## Technical structure helps search engines understand the site too

The project used the normal technical foundations I expect from a modern content site: canonical URLs, sitemap, semantic metadata, localized metadata where applicable and structured data that describes the page accurately.

Those mechanisms do not create relevance on their own. They reduce ambiguity for crawlers.

A technically perfect empty page is still an empty page.

## JSON-LD is description, not a ranking trick

Structured data is useful when it represents what the page actually contains.

I treat it as a machine-readable contract, not hidden SEO copy.

If a page describes a professional or an article, the structured representation should agree with visible content.

The same rule I use in APIs applies here: duplicated representations of the same fact should not drift.

## The blog needed to be operable by the professional

A content system fails if publishing requires a developer every time.

The public blog can benefit from static generation or revalidation, while an administrative interface handles drafts and publishing workflows.

The architecture goal is to keep the public reading path fast and simple without making the content owner depend on Git for ordinary editorial work.

## The admin area should not become search content

Private administrative routes need authentication and should not be indexed.

This sounds obvious, but SEO work is partly about knowing what **not** to expose.

Public articles, service pages and professional information are discoverable content. Draft editors, dashboards and internal actions are not.

## Health data raises the level of care

The LGPD classifies data concerning health as sensitive personal data.

That matters even for a small service website because a harmless-looking form can start collecting more context than the system actually needs.

My default is data minimization: if the purpose of the form is to request contact, do not turn it into a clinical intake form unless there is a justified workflow and appropriate controls for that data.

## A contact form is not a medical record

This distinction helped simplify the product.

For an initial contact, the system may only need identity, contact channel and a short message.

Collecting diagnosis, medication, detailed symptoms and family history "because it may be useful later" creates extra sensitivity and extra responsibility before the therapeutic process has even started.

Good architecture sometimes means refusing unnecessary data.

## Demo mode solved a development and presentation problem

A public-facing project often needs to be demonstrated without depending on real production data.

I like a demo mode that uses explicit mock/sample content instead of silently falling back to fake data during a real production failure.

Those are different behaviors.

Demo mode is intentional. Production failure should be observable.

## Failing clearly is better than pretending success

If scheduling or a write operation fails, the interface should say so.

A friendly fake "success" state might improve a screenshot, but it damages trust and creates operational inconsistency.

For high-trust products, truthful failure states are part of UX.

## Core Web Vitals are not just engineering trivia

A slow or unstable page can hurt both search visibility and the user experience.

But I avoid treating one local Lighthouse run as real-user truth. Field metrics such as LCP, INP and CLS depend on actual devices, networks and traffic.

The useful workflow is to build for performance, monitor field data and investigate regressions instead of chasing one synthetic score.

## Advertising guidance can evolve

Professional rules are not something I want hardcoded forever into a design assumption.

The site's content policy should be revisited when the CFP or regional councils update guidance.

For example, the CFP's 2022 technical note discusses professional advertising and cautions against sensationalism, guaranteed results and certain uses of testimonials and images. Those details deserve primary-source verification when content changes.

Regulated communication is a living product requirement.

## The biggest lesson was that ethics can improve UX

At first, the constraints looked like things the site could not do.

In practice, they pushed the product toward better decisions:

- explain instead of promise;
- identify instead of performing authority;
- inform instead of pressure;
- collect less data;
- make the next step clear.

The site tries to convert by reducing uncertainty.

That idea goes beyond psychology. In any high-trust service, the best CTA is sometimes not the loudest one. It is the one that appears after the person has enough information to decide for themselves.
